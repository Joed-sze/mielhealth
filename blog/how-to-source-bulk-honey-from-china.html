1. Why Source Bulk Honey from China?

China has been the world's largest honey producer for decades, accounting for approximately 25-30% of global honey output. In 2024, China produced an estimated 44-45 million tons of honey and exported over 169,000 tons of natural honey, representing an 11% year-over-year increase in export volume. For importers, food manufacturers, and trading companies looking for consistent, large-volume supply, China offers unmatched scale and reliability.

Several factors make China the go-to sourcing destination for bulk honey:

  • Unmatched Production Scale: With over 9 million managed bee colonies and a beekeeping tradition spanning millennia, China's honey production infrastructure is unparalleled. No other country can match the volume consistency required for large-scale industrial and retail supply chains.
  • Diverse Varietal Offerings: China's vast geography and climate diversity produce a wide range of monofloral honeys — from light, mild acacia honey to robust, dark buckwheat honey — giving buyers the flexibility to source specific varieties for different market segments.
  • Competitive Pricing: China's efficient beekeeping practices, particularly the migratory beekeeping model that follows seasonal nectar flows across provinces, keep production costs low. Combined with established export infrastructure at major ports, Chinese bulk honey offers exceptional value.
  • Established Export Infrastructure: Decades of honey export experience mean Chinese suppliers understand international quality standards, documentation requirements, and logistics. Major honey-producing provinces have dedicated testing laboratories and certification bodies.
  • Strategic Port Access: Key honey-producing regions in central and eastern China are well-connected to major ports including Shanghai, Ningbo, Qingdao, and Tianjin, ensuring efficient container loading and competitive ocean freight rates.

The primary export markets for Chinese honey are Japan, the European Union, and Southeast Asian countries. Japan alone accounts for a significant share of Chinese honey exports, reflecting the high quality standards that Chinese exporters routinely meet. In recent years, demand from Middle Eastern and African markets has also grown substantially.

Key Takeaway: China's combination of production scale, varietal diversity, competitive pricing, and mature export infrastructure makes it the most practical sourcing destination for bulk honey buyers worldwide. The key is working with an experienced exporter who understands both Chinese production standards and your destination market's regulatory requirements.

2. Chinese Honey Varieties and Producing Regions

One of China's greatest advantages for honey buyers is the incredible diversity of varietal honeys available. Understanding the major varieties and their producing regions will help you select the right honey for your market.

Major Producing Regions

Region Key Provinces Primary Varieties Characteristics

Key Varieties for Importers

Acacia Honey (洋槐蜜): Light-colored, mild-flavored, and slow to crystallize. Acacia honey is the most popular Chinese export variety, particularly favored in European and Japanese markets. Its high fructose content keeps it liquid for extended periods, making it ideal for retail packaging.

Linden Honey (椴树蜜): Produced primarily in Northeast China's Changbai Mountain region, linden honey has a distinctive minty aroma and pale amber color. It crystallizes into a fine, creamy texture and commands premium pricing in gourmet markets.

Rapeseed Honey (油菜蜜): The highest-volume variety in China, rapeseed honey is light amber with a mild, slightly floral taste. It crystallizes quickly and is widely used in industrial food processing, baking, and as a blending base.

Vitex Honey (荆条蜜): Produced in central and northern China, vitex honey has a medium amber color and a balanced, pleasant flavor. It is a versatile mid-range option suitable for both retail and industrial applications.

Buckwheat Honey (荞麦蜜): Dark, robust, and high in antioxidants, buckwheat honey has a strong, molasses-like flavor. It is popular in health food markets and for nutraceutical applications.

Jujube Honey (枣花蜜): Produced mainly in Northwest China, jujube honey has a deep amber color and a rich, fruity flavor. It is increasingly popular in Middle Eastern and health-conscious markets.

Key Takeaway: When sourcing bulk honey from China, buyers should specify the desired variety, region of origin, and target quality parameters. Each variety has distinct color, flavor, crystallization behavior, and market positioning. Working with a supplier who can source from multiple regions gives you the flexibility to meet diverse customer demands.

3. Quality Standards: What Every Buyer Must Know

Honey quality is determined by a combination of chemical, physical, and sensory parameters. Understanding these metrics is essential for specifying your requirements, evaluating supplier COAs, and ensuring your shipment meets destination market regulations.

Core Quality Parameters

Parameter China Standard (GB 14963) EU Standard What It Tells You
Moisture ≤ 23% (general); ≤ 20% (premium) ≤ 20% (general); ≤ 18% (heather) Lower moisture = less risk of fermentation. Buyers should specify ≤ 18% for export.
HMF (Hydroxymethylfurfural) ≤ 40 mg/kg ≤ 40 mg/kg (non-tropical); ≤ 80 mg/kg (tropical) Indicator of heat exposure and aging. Fresh, properly stored honey should be ≤ 15 mg/kg.
Enzyme Activity (Diastase) ≥ 8 mL/(g·h); ≥ 3 for low-enzyme varieties ≥ 8 (general); ≥ 3 (low-enzyme) Measures freshness and minimal heat processing. Higher values indicate better quality.
Sucrose ≤ 5% ≤ 5% Detects adulteration with cane sugar. Pure honey should be ≤ 5%, often ≤ 2%.
Fructose + Glucose ≥ 60% ≥ 60% Total reducing sugars. Higher values indicate purer honey.
Water Insoluble Solids ≤ 0.1% ≤ 0.1% Measures cleanliness and filtration quality.
Acidity ≤ 40 meq/kg ≤ 50 meq/kg Natural acidity from organic acids. Excessive acidity may indicate fermentation.

Additional Testing Parameters

Beyond the core quality metrics, buyers should consider requesting the following tests depending on destination market requirements:

  • Antibiotic Residues: Screen for chloramphenicol, nitrofurans, tetracyclines, sulfonamides, and streptomycin. The EU and Japan have zero-tolerance policies for many antibiotic residues. This is the single most common cause of honey shipment rejection.
  • Heavy Metals: Lead ≤ 1.0 mg/kg, arsenic ≤ 0.5 mg/kg. Some markets require additional testing for cadmium and mercury.
  • Pesticide Residues: Multi-residue screening for neonicotinoids, organophosphates, and other common agricultural chemicals.
  • Pollen Analysis: Determines the botanical and geographical origin of honey. Important for verifying monofloral claims.
  • C4 Sugar Adulteration: Carbon isotope ratio analysis (AOAC 998.12) to detect adulteration with corn syrup or cane sugar.
  • GMO Screening: Required for EU organic certification and some retail markets.

Key Takeaway: Always specify your quality requirements in writing before placing an order. Include target ranges for moisture, HMF, enzyme activity, and antibiotic residues. Request a pre-shipment COA and consider independent third-party testing for your first order. A reputable supplier will welcome this level of scrutiny.

4. Step-by-Step Procurement Process

Sourcing bulk honey from China follows a structured process. Understanding each step will help you plan your timeline, budget, and quality assurance strategy.

Step 1: Define Your Requirements

Before contacting suppliers, clearly define your needs: honey variety, annual volume, target quality parameters, packaging preference (drums, flexitanks, or IBC totes), destination market, and target price range. The more specific your requirements, the more accurate the quotations you will receive.

Step 2: Request Quotations and Samples

Contact 2-3 established exporters with your specifications. Request a detailed quotation including FOB price, MOQ, lead time, and payment terms. Ask for a 500g-1kg sample of the specific honey batch type you are considering. Sample evaluation should include sensory assessment (color, aroma, taste, texture) and ideally your own laboratory testing.

Step 3: Agree on Specifications and Contract

Once you have selected a supplier and evaluated samples, agree on a detailed product specification sheet. This should include all quality parameters, acceptable ranges, testing methods, packaging specifications, and delivery schedule. The purchase contract should specify payment terms (typically 30% deposit, 70% before shipment), Incoterms (FOB or CIF), and penalties for non-compliance.

Step 4: Pre-Shipment Inspection and Testing

Before the shipment leaves the factory, request a pre-shipment COA covering all agreed parameters. For first-time orders or large shipments, consider hiring a third-party inspection company (such as SGS, Intertek, or Bureau Veritas) to verify the shipment against specifications. This small investment can prevent costly disputes later.

Step 5: Shipping and Logistics

Bulk honey is typically shipped in 290 kg steel drums or flexitanks (20-24 tons per 20-foot container). Ocean freight is the standard shipping method for bulk orders. Transit time from Shanghai or Ningbo to major European ports is 25-35 days; to the US West Coast, 15-20 days.

Step 6: Customs Clearance

Upon arrival, your customs broker will handle clearance using the documentation package provided by the exporter. The shipment may be subject to physical inspection and sampling by your country's food safety authority. Having complete, accurate documentation significantly reduces the risk of clearance delays.

Key Takeaway: The procurement process from initial inquiry to delivery typically takes 8-12 weeks for a first-time order. Building a relationship with a reliable supplier streamlines this timeline significantly for repeat orders, often reducing it to 4-6 weeks.

5. Import Documentation and Customs Clearance

Proper documentation is critical for smooth customs clearance. Missing or incomplete paperwork is one of the most common causes of shipment delays and additional costs.

Essential Export Documents from China

  • Certificate of Origin (COO): Issued by China's customs authority or CCPIT, confirming the honey's Chinese origin. Essential for tariff classification and preferential trade agreements.
  • Health Certificate / Sanitary Certificate: Issued by China's Entry-Exit Inspection and Quarantine Bureau (CIQ), confirming the honey meets food safety standards and is fit for human consumption.
  • Certificate of Analysis (COA): Detailed laboratory test results for the specific batch, covering all agreed quality parameters.
  • Commercial Invoice: Itemized invoice showing product description, quantity, unit price, total value, and Incoterms.
  • Packing List: Detailed breakdown of packaging, including number of drums, net weight, gross weight, and container information.
  • Bill of Lading (B/L): The transport document issued by the shipping line, serving as proof of shipment and ownership.

Destination Country Requirements

Requirements vary by country, but common additional documents needed at the destination include:

  • EU: Honey must come from an approved third-country establishment listed in the EU's TRACES system. Organic honey requires additional certification from an EU-recognized organic control body.
  • USA: FDA Prior Notice must be submitted before arrival. The importing company must have an FDA food facility registration. Honey is subject to USDA grading and labeling requirements.
  • Japan: Strict antibiotic residue testing, particularly for chloramphenicol and nitrofurans. Import notification must be filed with the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare.
  • Middle East: Halal certification and a Certificate of Free Sale may be required. Some Gulf countries require additional attestation from the Arab-Brazilian Chamber of Commerce or equivalent.

Key Takeaway: Work with a supplier who provides a complete documentation package for every shipment. Verify that your customs broker is familiar with honey imports from China specifically, as honey is subject to unique regulatory requirements that differ from general food imports.

6. Common Mistakes When Importing Bulk Honey

Even experienced importers can make costly errors when sourcing honey from China. Here are the most common pitfalls and how to avoid them:

  • Not specifying moisture content. Chinese GB standards allow up to 23% moisture, but most export markets require ≤ 18-20%. Always specify your moisture requirement clearly. Honey with moisture above 20% risks fermentation during transit, especially in warm conditions.
  • Ignoring antibiotic residue testing. Antibiotic residues are the #1 cause of honey shipment rejection worldwide. Chinese beekeepers do not systematically use antibiotics, but contamination can occur. Always require multi-residue screening as part of the COA, and consider independent verification for your first order.
  • Choosing the wrong packaging for your climate. Steel drums with food-grade epoxy lining are the standard. Flexitanks are cost-effective for large volumes but require careful handling. For hot climate destinations, insulated container liners are worth the additional cost to prevent quality degradation.
  • Not verifying the honey variety. "Acacia honey" is a common claim, but true monofloral acacia honey requires pollen analysis verification. Some suppliers may blend varieties to meet a price point. Request pollen analysis if monofloral claims are important for your market positioning.
  • Underestimating lead times. Honey production is seasonal, and specific varieties may only be available during certain harvest windows. Place orders well in advance, especially for single-variety honey from specific regions. Late-season orders may face limited availability or higher prices.
  • Paying 100% upfront to an unverified supplier. Standard trade terms are 30% deposit with order and 70% balance against shipping documents. Never pay in full before verifying the supplier's track record and preferably visiting their facility or engaging a third-party verification service.

Key Takeaway: The most common honey import failures stem from inadequate quality specifications, insufficient testing, and poor supplier due diligence. A clear specification sheet, pre-shipment COA, and verified supplier credentials are your three strongest protections.
